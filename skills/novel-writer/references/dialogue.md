# Character-Owned Dialogue

Use this reference for any scene with sustained spoken exchange or for dialogue that feels stiff, overly logical, interchangeable, translated, expositional, or uniformly witty.

## 1. Two anchors per speaker

Before drafting, give each important speaker only two voice anchors:

- **Vocabulary source** — age, household, job, money, food, illness, tools, school, military life, faith, street, bureaucracy, or another lived domain.
- **Permission with this listener** — can order, tease, refuse, correct, conceal, explain, plead, or leave something unanswered.

Do not assign catchphrases, sentence-length quotas, interruption counts, or prepared clever lines. Character voice is a pattern of attention and permission, not an accent sticker.

## 2. Write what reaches the mouth first

People often begin with the part they most need heard:

- answer;
- request;
- refusal;
- complaint;
- warning;
- joke;
- silence or action instead of an answer.

Add reasons when the character wants to persuade, save face, discharge a duty, or has been pressed. Stop before the person turns their reaction into a complete essay about their psychology and the story's theme.

Colloquial speech does not mean fragments. A parent giving household instructions, a medic reporting a condition, a diplomat protecting wording, or a teacher answering sincerely may use a complete long sentence.

## 2A. Meaning is not yet spoken language

A line can be grammatically valid and semantically decodable yet still be something this person would not say here. Before keeping a corrected line, pass four gates:

1. **Live target** — what action, answer, object, or decision does the speaker want changed now?
2. **Spoken chunk** — would this age, relationship, and register normally package the words in this order?
3. **Ordinary verb** — does the verb name the result people in this work or household actually ask for, rather than an abstract motion or after-the-fact description?
4. **Recoverable omission** — if a noun or verb is omitted, can the listener and reader recover one clear referent from the nearby scene?

Read the turn aloud in the target language. Do not accept “the reader can work out what it means” as proof of natural speech. If understanding requires silently restoring the author's missing object, technical intention, or full logical chain, restore the ordinary spoken chunk instead of adding filler.

## 2B. Speech is a lossy social act

Do not pour a character's complete private model into dialogue. Convert it:

```text
what I think or fear → what I need from you now
→ what I believe you will hear → what I can risk saying
→ the spoken remainder
```

The loss comes from purpose and relationship, not from a quota of fragments or evasions. Keep the necessary object and ordinary verb. A speaker may expose a full chain when persuading formally, confessing deliberately, giving evidence, or controlling a public record.

Mechanical private-state transcript:

> “I know I act indifferent because I am afraid you will leave and discover that you no longer need me.”

Possible sibling exchange when the older brother cannot ask her to stay:

> “钥匙拿上。”
>
> “我明天回来。”
>
> “我说钥匙。”

The exchange does not prove the hidden motive. It lets the present object, repeated request, and refusal to answer the reassurance carry it. If the scene has not established that relationship pressure, use a plainer request rather than importing this pattern.

## 3. Let listeners hear differently

A response follows what the listener cares about, understands, fears, or is permitted to acknowledge. It may answer fully, answer one part, correct a small detail, change the subject, continue the task, or misunderstand.

Do not force selective hearing in every exchange. Direct answers are natural when people cooperate, time is short, the relationship is safe, or the fact is simple.

## 3A. Build the listener model

The speaker does not address the listener as the author knows them. The speaker addresses a possibly wrong model of them. For an important exchange, ask:

```text
I think you already know ___;
you will probably accept / challenge / misunderstand ___;
I need you to ___;
I cannot let you conclude ___;
so I begin with ___ and stop before ___.
```

This makes the same fact sound different with a caregiver, friend, rival, subordinate, stranger, or enemy without assigning fixed voices to relationship categories. Let previous favors, broken promises, ordinary work, and failed repairs alter what can be omitted or demanded. A mistaken listener model may itself cause conflict; do not make every speaker socially omniscient.

## 3B. Divide group reactions by permission

When several people witness the same arrival, injury, revelation, or ordinary purchase, do not distribute one reaction template across the cast. First ask what each person can socially or practically take over: a body, wound, meal, door, tool, child, record, payment, explanation, or nothing yet.

Let the person with real permission act first. Others may notice a different consequence, arrive late, misunderstand, keep working, or stay silent. Equal concern does not require equal questions, equal insight, or equal page space. Distinction comes from **response jurisdiction**—what this person is used to handling with these people—not from assigning four different facial expressions.

## 4. Let shared life carry premises

Friends, family, coworkers, and unit members do not restate facts they both live with. Use the words they would need to change the present action. Readers may temporarily understand only part of a private reference, but the nearby object, action, or consequence must make the essential meaning recoverable.

Avoid fake omission: deleting necessary objects and replacing them with “that thing,” “the matter,” or unexplained jargon does not create subtext.

## 5. Common symptoms and repairs

| Symptom | Evidence | Minimal repair |
|---|---|---|
| Everyone speaks in short outline fragments | Sentence length and cadence are uniform across roles and relationships | Restore natural sentence blocks for caregivers, professionals, formal speakers, or anyone who needs to explain |
| Premise–reason–conclusion speech | Several turns fully argue what ordinary talk would leave assumed | Put the answer or request first; remove reasons the speaker gains nothing from saying |
| Shared author intelligence | Children, workers, rulers, and outsiders use the same abstractions and diagnose motives accurately | Return each speaker to visible facts, trained knowledge, biased guesses, and social risk |
| Information relay | Characters take turns asking exactly the questions required by the setting outline | Let one person pursue their own task; move necessary facts into action, consequence, or a motivated explanation |
| Reciprocal completeness | Every objection is answered point by point and every question closes | Allow postponement, partial agreement, practical interruption, or a conflict that remains socially unresolved |
| Universal wit | Every line lands a comeback, aphorism, or punchline | Keep the turn that changes behavior; let other lines be plain, missed, or imperfectly timed |
| Therapy language | Characters state another person's trauma, avoidance, and true desire without earned evidence | Use an observation, a possibly wrong question, or concrete help |
| Plot-aware questions | A speaker asks about information they have no present reason to want | Give the question a personal stake or remove it |
| Pseudo-specialized naming | Ordinary tools, roads, lessons, or chores receive compound names that no community needs | Use the local plain term until distinction becomes operationally necessary |
| Coordinate ending | A closing exchange recites time, place, equipment, and objective like a quest log | Answer only the live question; let prior action carry the rest |
| Action-tag choreography | Every line is followed by a glance, lip movement, cup, fist, or breath | Keep tags for speaker clarity, causal action, bodily interference, or a parallel task |
| Translated light-novel voice | Chinese dialogue copies Japanese fillers, honorific logic, ellipses, or explanatory self-address | Preserve the interaction mechanism while rebuilding idiomatic Chinese syntax and social distance |
| Catchphrase identity | Removing a repeated filler erases the only difference between speakers | Differentiate what each notices, risks, withholds, and asks from this listener |
| Dialogue closes the theme | The final line states what the scene meant for both people | End on an answer, refusal, action, practical task, or silence that leaves interpretation alive |
| Generic evaluation reply | A child or subordinate shows concrete work and receives only “good,” “stable,” or “well done” from any authority figure | Let the relationship continue through the next real task, a specific concern, or the receiver taking over the work |
| Written clause order | Familiar speech presents premise, reason, condition, location, and conclusion in explanatory order | Put the answer, request, or practical condition where this listener expects it; remove reasons that do no social work |
| Overcompressed orality | “Naturalization” turns everyone into two-beat outline fragments and drops necessary nouns or relationship warmth | Restore the complete spoken chunk this role and relationship support; preserve genuinely complete short answers |
| Weak task verb | A request names a temporary motion when the speaker wants a completed repair or chore | Use the ordinary verb for the desired result; keep the temporary verb only when temporary holding is the real task |
| Visible-action translation | A character restates an already visible action with vague direction such as “it went backward” | Delete the line or name the concrete danger and affected body or object if warning is necessary |
| Tidy mishap accounting | Casual old embarrassment becomes precise point-by-point damage accounting with no present consequence | Keep blame, cost, or refusal that matters now; compress irrelevant counts and explanations |
| Punchline tail | An ordinary reminder adds a simile, antithesis, rhetorical question, or callback only to land neatly | Remove the tail and retain what the speaker wants done; keep humor only when shared history changes the exchange |
| Voice context fracture | Individual lines sound colloquial, but one character's attention, permission, and face-saving reset between scenes | Restore current task, listener permission, vocabulary source, and pressure response before touching fillers |
| World-time register drift | Characters use clocks, dates, ranks, titles, or measures inconsistent with established daily and official practice | Retrieve the world's actual register and distinguish formal record from household speech |
| Common-item group reveal | A cheap ordinary purchase makes the whole cast ask price, request inspection, and wait for a delayed reveal | Limit attention to users or materially interested people; let the object gain weight through later use |
| Semantic but unsaid line | The sentence can be paraphrased, but its verb, clause order, or missing object does not belong to this speaker's ordinary spoken chunk | Rebuild from the live target, ordinary verb, and recoverable shared premise; do not merely add particles |
| Group reaction cloning | Several characters react to one event with the same question, warning, insight, or concern plus different gestures | Assign response jurisdiction; let one take over, one notice another consequence, one lag, and one remain occupied or silent |

A single complete sentence, direct answer, repeated word, em dash, or action tag is not evidence by itself.

## 6. Instruction, briefing, and exposition

First determine the instructor's real topic and normal stopping point. A teacher can answer directly, refuse, be wrong, continue the planned lesson, ask for an object, or admit lack of experience.

Avoid these default shapes:

- student asks one catalog question per setting category;
- instructor responds with a three-part universal principle;
- learner fails exactly once;
- rule is explained;
- learner immediately succeeds and proves the lesson;
- the final accident neatly demonstrates today's theme.

Keep only questions that arise from what the student is holding, doing, fearing, misunderstanding, or about to attempt. Summarize routine instruction in narration; quote corrections, refusals, personal experience, relationship friction, and words that change the next action.

## 7. Original repair examples

### Over-complete teenager

Mechanical:

> “I understand that you are worried about my safety, but I have already considered the risks and I need you to respect my decision.”

Possible repair, stubborn younger brother to older sister:

> “I already took the money.”
>
> “Give it back.”
>
> “No.”

This is not a universal short-line template. The situation supports it because the concrete commitment and sibling permission carry the argument.

### Direct emergency cooperation

> “How many at the east gate?”
>
> “Six. Two can't walk.”
>
> “Bring the four down first. I'll send the stretcher back.”

Do not add evasion or personality flourishes merely to make this less efficient. Competence and urgency justify clear speech.

### Formal speaker

> “If we open the granary tonight, the lower quarter eats for three days and the army loses a week of reserve. I will sign that order, but not while your regiment still has flour hidden in the west depot.”

A complete argument belongs here because the speaker is bargaining publicly, controlling the record, and attaching responsibility.

## 8. Voice continuity

Across scenes, maintain the same underlying vocabulary sources and relationship differences while allowing mood and purpose to change surface rhythm. A character may be concise in danger and rambling at home without becoming a different author.

When dialogue fails, repair in this order:

1. oral order;
2. live target and listener model;
3. relationship permission and microhistory;
4. vocabulary source and experience residue;
5. information boundary;
6. sentence rhythm.

Do not begin by adding fillers, profanity, ellipses, stutters, slang, or interruptions.

## 9. Final read

Read the whole exchange, not isolated lines:

1. What does each speaker want the listener to do now?
2. Are several speakers using the same level of abstraction?
3. Does anyone explain more than their relationship or purpose requires?
4. Can key nouns and actions be recovered without author notes?
5. Are tags performing real work?
6. If names are hidden, can the relationship positions still be inferred from permission and information?
7. Is every omitted object uniquely recoverable, and would the target language actually omit it here?

Report or repair at most one dialogue pattern per revision pass unless the author explicitly asks for a full dialogue workshop.
