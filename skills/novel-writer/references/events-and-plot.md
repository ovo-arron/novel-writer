# Events and Plot Bible

Use this reference to design, organize, expand, sequence, or audit story events and plot. An event is a change in the fictional world. Plot is the selected causal path by which readers encounter changes, choices, revelations, and consequences.

## 1. Separate event from presentation

Keep four questions distinct:

1. What happened in world time?
2. Who caused, prevented, witnessed, misunderstood, concealed, or recorded it?
3. When and through whose viewpoint does the reader learn it?
4. What later action changes because of it?

The same event may be shown directly, reported badly, reconstructed later, remembered falsely, or remain outside viewpoint. A revelation is not automatically a new event; it may be a new interpretation of an old one.

## 2. Event types and authority

| Type | Meaning |
|---|---|
| Historical fact | occurred before the present narrative and has current consequences |
| Manuscript event | occurred in accepted prose; highest event authority |
| Author-confirmed event | explicitly fixed by the author but not necessarily written yet |
| Planned event | intended future beat; non-canonical until written or confirmed as fact |
| Alternate event | belongs only to a forecast or branch |
| Rumored event | exists as an in-world claim, not objective truth |
| False account | deliberately or mistakenly contradicts what happened |

Never merge planned, rumored, or alternate events into canon summaries.

## 3. Event record

Use only fields needed for retrieval:

```text
Event name or plain identifier:
Status and authority:
World time and duration:
Location:
Entry conditions:
Participants and separate aims:
Trigger:
Attempts and resistance:
Decisive choice or failure:
Immediate result:
Material and relational aftermath:
Knowledge spread: who knows what, from which source
Resources, injuries, promises, debts, or status changed:
Linked world rules, items, and species:
Open threads created, advanced, transformed, or resolved:
Required reader-experienced movements:
Estimated chapter span and reason:
Actual chapter span and compression/expansion note:
Manuscript or author source:
```

For a minor event, one or two sentences may be enough. Do not turn ordinary life into database administration.

## 4. Causal plot construction

Prefer chains in which solutions produce later pressure:

```text
need → choice → opposition response → cost → altered options
→ next choice under changed conditions
```

Useful causal links include:

- a promise creates a deadline;
- victory consumes supplies or legitimacy;
- concealment protects someone and corrupts later trust;
- training grants competence and attracts duty;
- rescue changes public identity;
- a law solves one abuse and creates another;
- an item changes custody and therefore power;
- ecological disruption changes settlement or war.

Coincidence may start trouble. Character choice should usually determine how trouble develops or what it costs.

## 5. Timeline and sequence

Maintain world chronology separately from manuscript order. For events whose timing matters, record start, duration, travel and message delays, overlaps, uncertainty, and the earliest each participant could know or respond.

Use a timeline to catch impossibility, not to force every chapter into equal intervals. Approximate dates may remain approximate. If sources disagree, preserve the competing dates and their authority instead of averaging them.

## 6. Character and faction participation

Before fixing a major event, check:

- each participant's immediate aim;
- knowledge and false assumptions at entry;
- authority, body, resources, and relationship constraints;
- what action remains available if the preferred plan fails;
- which outcome each side can accept;
- how an apparently shared victory is distributed unequally.

Do not make supporting characters arrive in plot order solely to provide tools, answers, witnesses, or emotional approval. An institution or faction should continue pursuing its future between protagonist appearances.

## 7. Information and revelation

For mysteries, conspiracies, hidden identities, or political plots, track:

| Layer | Question |
|---|---|
| Reality | What happened? |
| Evidence | What traces exist, and what else could they mean? |
| Access | Who could encounter or alter the evidence? |
| Belief | What does each relevant character currently think? |
| Incentive | Who wants belief to change or remain false? |
| Action | What decision changes before certainty arrives? |

A clue should alter risk, trust, timing, or available action before the final answer. Avoid clues that exist only to prove the planner was clever.

## 8. Arc, volume, chapter, and scene

- **Story**: the durable transformation and central pressure across the whole work.
- **Arc**: a sequence of related choices and consequences that changes a capability, relationship, institution, or public condition.
- **Volume**: a readable promise with a practical objective and a consequential settlement.
- **Chapter**: a unit of expectation, pressure, discovery, action, or aftermath with a changed exit state.
- **Scene**: continuous action under a local objective, place/time relation, and resistance.

Do not force each level to repeat the same structure at a smaller scale. A chapter may be preparation or aftermath; a scene may establish a routine later pressure will break.

## 9. Pacing and aftermath

Pacing is the management of expectation, change, and processing time—not action density alone.

Vary:

- distance between question and answer;
- size and reversibility of consequences;
- viewpoint access;
- practical work required before the next attempt;
- relationship processing time;
- public versus private consequences;
- preparation, collision, and aftermath.

After a major event, decide what continues as body, debt, cleanup, record, rumor, shortage, altered route, duty, grief, fear, or opportunity. Do not reset the story after the dramatic paragraph.

## 10. Event span and chapter allocation

Read `plot-and-pacing-engine.md` when deciding how much page space an event deserves. Do not map “one event” to “one chapter” automatically: an event may be a single turn inside a chapter, while an evacuation, trial, siege, investigation, or negotiation may contain several causally distinct movements.

First identify what readers must experience for the outcome to feel earned: preparation, attempt, countermove, evidence, reinterpretation, relationship permission, logistical or institutional process, decisive choice, and aftermath. Merge movements that share one operative question and causal direction. Split when compression would conceal agency, opposition, knowledge acquisition, changed permission, or consequential residue.

Assign every planned chapter a changed exit state. If two neighboring chapters produce the same state change, one is probably rehearsal or padding. If one chapter must carry several unrelated decisions, revelations, location changes, and aftermaths, the event is probably rushed. Use chapter counts as revisable forecasts; settle actual spans only after accepted prose.

## 11. Plot audit

Check:

1. Which choice makes this event happen now?
2. Could the same event occur unchanged with different characters? If yes, restore motive and constraint.
3. Does opposition respond with its own knowledge and resources?
4. Did a plan, rumor, or reader inference accidentally become fact?
5. Does the outcome change later options?
6. Is a revelation supported by evidence and access?
7. Are time, travel, communication, injury, money, and custody possible?
8. Did the chapter add a twist because its actual changed state felt too quiet?
9. Do several scenes merely fill worldbuilding, character, training, and clue slots without causal carryover?
10. What remains unfinished after the apparent resolution?

Repair the earliest broken causal link. Do not compensate with a larger villain, surprise attack, or unexplained secret.
