# Living Characters: Biography, Relationship, Choice, and OOC

Use this reference when characters have detailed cards but still feel interchangeable, when background does not affect present behavior, when dialogue ignores relationship history, or when a surprising choice may be OOC. It deepens `characters.md`; it does not replace canon retrieval or project-specific voice evidence.

## 1. The runtime person

A living character is not the sum of traits. They are a bounded decision-maker whose present response grows from durable identity, accumulated experience, current state, and a particular listener.

```text
identity invariant
+ learned rules from experience
+ relationship-specific permission and listener model
+ current body, resources, knowledge, duty, and recent residue
+ live task and pressure
→ attention → available options → subjective choice → speech/action
→ cost → memory update
```

Retrieve only fields that can alter the scene. A complete dossier is not evidence of life. If the same attention, choice, and line survive a name swap, the person is not yet carrying the scene.

## 2. Turn history into residue

Do not insert biography as explanation. Ask what the person concluded when the experience happened and how that conclusion still saves time today.

```text
Experience:
What the person believed happened:
Learned rule or shortcut:
What now triggers it:
First thing noticed:
Automatic first move:
Where the rule is useful:
What it makes them miss or misprice:
Exception or breaking point:
Current manuscript evidence:
```

The learned rule may be practical: check the hinge before blaming the lock; count portions before sitting down; hide pain until the work is finished. It may be social: adults only believe visible results; asking twice creates debt; silence keeps a household calm. It need not be profound, correct, or consciously worded.

Use three kinds of residue:

- **procedural** — order of work, practiced shortcut, what receives a hand first;
- **social** — who may help, what counts as debt, shame, insult, loyalty, or abandonment;
- **interpretive** — which explanation arrives first and which evidence is discounted.

A single past event should not explain the whole person. Several experiences may support conflicting rules. Under pressure, the rule with the strongest trigger, relationship risk, or recent confirmation usually wins.

Do not force a flashback. Let residue appear through selection, timing, refusal, overpreparation, an omitted question, a familiar mistake, or the price the person agrees to pay.

## 3. Preserve identity through changing states

Separate durable identity from surface mode.

**Identity invariant** may include priority order, line resisted, default way of earning safety, desired social image, or what is protected when time runs out. It should survive several settings.

**Surface mode** changes with:

- pain, fatigue, hunger, intoxication, recovery, or age;
- public role versus private intimacy;
- authority, dependence, audience, shame, concealment, or urgency;
- recent victory, humiliation, grief, debt, promise, betrayal, or repair.

A talkative rival may fall silent before a caregiver because admitting the injury threatens the same image normally defended through boasting. A gentle caregiver may speak sharply in danger because keeping people alive outranks appearing gentle. The surface changes; the priority remains legible.

For the scene, state internally:

```text
Usually protects ___ by ___;
today ___ blocks that method;
with this listener they can / cannot ___;
therefore the same priority appears as ___.
```

Do not convert one frightened, formal, exhausted, or intimate scene into a permanent voice update unless the behavior repeats or the event durably changes the person.

## 4. Model the listener and relationship history

Characters speak to their model of the listener, not to the author's complete character card. For each important direction, record:

```text
Speaker believes listener already knows:
Speaker expects listener to accept, challenge, repeat, punish, or misread:
Speaker wants changed now:
Speaker must keep listener from concluding:
Permission: order / interrupt / touch / tease / refuse / leave / withhold
Explanation threshold:
```

Add only microhistory that changes present permission:

- favor, debt, gift, rescue, or withheld help;
- broken promise or routine disappointment;
- division of work and who normally cleans up;
- old embarrassment, nickname, form of address, or touch right;
- last conflict and whether the usual repair worked;
- topic one may raise and the other may not.

History should alter the turn without requiring recap. A hand removed from a sleeve, an object returned too carefully, a short “又来？” or a refused meal can carry more history than an explanatory paragraph. Ensure the nearby scene makes the essential meaning recoverable.

Relationships are directional. A may tease B while B cannot tease A; A may think a debt settled while B still acts as if it remains. Conflict often comes from different listener models rather than different facts.

## 5. Let background own attention and action

Background should change more than vocabulary and props. It should shape:

- what is noticed before danger is named;
- the categories used to divide a problem;
- which loss looks expensive, shameful, repairable, or ordinary;
- the first tool, person, or route trusted;
- tolerance for uncertainty, debt, delay, witnesses, and bodily risk;
- what the person assumes everyone already knows;
- where competence ends and overconfidence begins.

Three experienced people may enter the same damaged room and disagree before anyone speaks: one tests load and salvage, one protects records and order, one checks exits and ambush positions. Do not give each an equal demonstration paragraph. Let their work collide around the live task.

Professional competence remains bounded. A veteran may read a formation and misread a child. A merchant may price cargo and miss ritual meaning. A caregiver may recognize fever and misunderstand secrecy. Expertise creates characteristic blind spots as well as solutions.

## 6. Choose the subjective best option

Readers may know the better choice. The character only chooses among options they notice, believe possible, can bear socially, and can afford now.

Before a consequential choice, map:

```text
Options genuinely visible to the character:
Option dismissed immediately and why:
Risk the character overprices:
Risk the character underprices:
Debt, shame, loyalty, image, or duty altering the price:
Habitual strategy favored by past success:
Trigger making the choice necessary now:
```

A flawed choice is credible when it protects something real from inside the person. It becomes plot-forced stupidity when the character forgets available knowledge, ignores a cost they routinely notice, or refuses an obvious safe alternative without pressure.

After failure, let the person first explain it through their existing model. They may blame insufficient preparation, the wrong witness, bad timing, betrayal, or a specific technical mistake. Growth begins when accumulated evidence changes what they count as the problem, not when a mentor supplies the correct lesson.

## 7. Put the person into dialogue

Move from character state to spoken line in this order:

```text
live target → listener model → relationship permission
→ experience-owned vocabulary and ordinary spoken chunk
→ face-saving order → omission or explanation → bodily delivery
```

Do not write an ideal statement of motive and then colloquialize it. Decide what reaches the mouth first. A person may report the work already done, name the object at risk, ask for food, correct a small factual error, or keep doing the task instead of answering the emotional question.

The same speaker can use different lengths and registers while remaining recognizable. Continuity lies in attention, stakes, permission, and preferred protection—not a quota of fragments, particles, ellipses, jokes, or profanity.

For a key line, test:

1. Why speak now rather than act or stay silent?
2. What response does the speaker want from this listener?
3. What do they think the listener already understands?
4. What conclusion are they preventing?
5. Which word order and verb come from their household, trade, age, or role?
6. What would they say to a different listener, and why would it change?

## 8. Keep interiority biased

Characters need not understand themselves accurately. Separate:

- **self-model** — the label and reason they give themselves;
- **behavioral evidence** — repeated choices, attention, and cost;
- **social mirror** — what others notice, project, or misunderstand;
- **authorial truth** — which may remain unresolved.

Interiority should use the person's available concepts and self-protection. They may call care “finishing the job,” fear “being sensible,” envy “not trusting the method,” or grief “being tired.” Do not expose the discrepancy after every act. Repetition, consequence, and later choice can let the reader see more than the person admits.

Intelligence does not create perfect self-knowledge. A reflective character may analyze systems well and still misname a personal motive. A young or practical character can think clearly without adopting therapeutic or thematic language.

## 9. Classify OOC before repair

Do not use “surprising” and “OOC” as synonyms.

| Class | Evidence | Repair |
|---|---|---|
| Impossible | missing knowledge, ability, access, body, time, or resource | change action or conditions |
| Unsupported convenience | no supported value, pressure, strategy, blocked alternative, or aftermath | redesign the beat |
| Surprising but bridgeable | identity supports it, but trigger, escalation, transition, or visible option map is missing | add the smallest bridge |
| Earned development / role performance | prior pressure, rehearsal, concealment, cost, or social role supports it; residue persists | preserve and settle |

Then inspect the exact failure layer: knowledge, ability, motive, values, relationship permission, listener model, bodily state, experience residue, missing transition, or wording. Repair the smallest valid layer.

Do not rewrite the whole biography to justify one twist. Do not add a secret trauma after the fact. Do not preserve a desired action by placing a perfect psychological explanation immediately before it.

## 10. Keep side characters alive offscreen

A supporting character needs a future, not equal page space. At exit, retain:

```text
unfinished task or relationship pressure:
next action without protagonist involvement:
resource, belief, body, or permission likely to change:
point where this may cross or obstruct the main line:
what the protagonist cannot know yet:
```

On return, show one observable result before any summary. The plan may have failed or changed. Do not make every offscreen action secretly serve, watch, betray, or praise the protagonist.

## 11. Compact project memory

For recurring characters, Core III should prefer four executable records over long biography summaries:

1. **identity invariant** — what survives changing moods and roles;
2. **experience residue map** — experience, learned rule, trigger, first move, blind cost;
3. **listener-mode map** — directional permission, expectation, misread, explanation threshold;
4. **current delta** — body, knowledge, resources, debt, recent choice, and unresolved aftermath.

Keep source pointers. Mark inference and trial. Do not invent material merely to complete the form. Update only after author acceptance or accepted manuscript evidence.

## 12. Draft and revision tests

Use only the tests relevant to the scene:

1. **Name-swap** — can another character perform the same attention, choice, and line unchanged?
2. **History-removal** — if the background is removed, does present behavior remain identical?
3. **Listener-swap** — would the speaker tell a caregiver, rival, and stranger the same thing in the same order?
4. **Option visibility** — did the person choose among options they actually know and can afford?
5. **Self-model** — does interiority know more about motive than the person has earned?
6. **State/invariant** — did surface change erase durable priorities, or did fidelity flatten every mood?
7. **Competence boundary** — does experience solve only what it plausibly covers?
8. **Residue** — will the choice affect the next interaction, strategy, body, resource, or belief?
9. **Offscreen future** — what would this person do next if the protagonist vanished?

Failure at a deeper layer outranks sentence polish. Once the action and line belong to the person, stop adding quirks, gestures, slang, or explanatory psychology.
