# Worldbuilding Bible

Use this reference to create, expand, organize, or audit a fictional world. Worldbuilding is not a catalog of lore. It is the set of physical, social, economic, and political conditions that shape what people can do, what they believe is normal, and what consequences survive a scene.

Keep detailed operational rules in `systems.md`, specific organizational decisions in `factions.md`, and traversable layouts/routes in `locations.md`. Worldbuilding owns the macro context and cross-system consequences.

## 1. Build only the depth the story can use

Start with pressures already touching characters. Expand outward when a scene, event, relationship, journey, institution, resource, or future arc needs the answer. Do not fill every field before writing.

Every major rule should have:

1. an ordinary use;
2. a practical limit or cost;
3. people who benefit;
4. people who carry the cost;
5. a way the rule is enforced, evaded, misunderstood, or changed.

A fact may remain atmospheric. Not every custom, street, meal, or holiday needs a payoff.

## 2. World domains

Use only relevant domains:

| Domain | Questions that produce story |
|---|---|
| Physical world | What do climate, terrain, seasons, distance, disease, and natural hazards make difficult? |
| Peoples and languages | Who lives where, moves where, mixes, assimilates, or remains separated? What is mutually understood, formally written, or locally named? |
| States and borders | Who claims rule, who actually governs, and where does authority weaken? |
| Law and administration | Who records births, land, debt, crime, military duty, magic use, inheritance, and travel? |
| Economy | What is produced, transported, taxed, scarce, hoarded, repaired, inherited, or stolen? |
| Class and status | What grants respect, protection, education, mobility, or legal personhood? |
| Military and security | Who can use force, who supplies it, and what happens away from the battlefield? |
| Religion and cosmology | Which practices organize time, legitimacy, death, taboo, charity, and conflict? |
| Education and knowledge | Who can read, train, certify, copy, teach, research, or suppress information? |
| Family and daily life | Who raises children, owns property, performs care, chooses marriage, and survives illness? |
| Magic and technology | What becomes possible, expensive, dangerous, regulated, or socially unequal? |
| History and memory | Which past events are documented, ritualized, denied, mythologized, or remembered differently? |

## 3. State and political power

Do not define a country only by ruler title and map color. Record:

- claimed territory versus controlled territory;
- source of legitimacy: ancestry, election, conquest, religion, examination, wealth, protection, or custom;
- institutions that turn orders into action;
- tax, census, courts, police, military recruitment, and local intermediaries;
- factions that want different futures;
- succession and emergency rules;
- groups inside the state that receive unequal rights or protection;
- what officials know late, falsely, or only on paper;
- what ordinary people do when state capacity fails.

Political conflict becomes plot when two actors attempt incompatible policies with limited information and real constituencies. Avoid making an entire country share one personality or moral position.

## 4. Economy and material life

An economy is a chain of labor, material, transport, permission, exchange, and risk.

For a resource that matters, trace:

```text
source → labor → processing → storage → transport → buyer or allocator
→ tax or theft → waste, repair, reuse, or shortage
```

Check currency only where exchange needs precision. Barter, credit, obligation, rationing, wages, household pooling, state allotment, and black markets may coexist.

Let prices and shortages change action. A rare medicine matters through who can obtain it, how long it travels, what substitutes exist, and who goes without—not through a number alone.

## 5. Culture, institutions, and ordinary life

Culture appears through permission and routine:

- who may speak first, touch whom, inherit, travel, study, own weapons, or refuse an order;
- how people address kin, strangers, superiors, children, clergy, soldiers, and the dead;
- meals, sanitation, housing, clothing, work hours, childcare, courtship, mourning, festivals, and rest;
- what is shameful, admirable, private, negotiable, sacred, or simply inconvenient;
- differences by region, class, generation, profession, and household.

Do not write culture as a list of exotic customs. Let a rule affect a request, delay, meal, document, body, relationship, or cost.

## 6. Rules, magic, technology, and information

For any important system, record:

- observable effect;
- required material, knowledge, body, permission, time, or environment;
- failure modes and uncertainty;
- training and verification;
- maintenance and repair;
- law, ownership, trade, and military use;
- ordinary uses outside combat;
- who misunderstands it and why;
- what it cannot solve.

Characters know rules at different levels. Separate formal theory, trade practice, household belief, propaganda, rumor, and lived exception. Do not let a system become a clean game interface unless the world actually uses one.

## 7. History and contested memory

For a historical event, distinguish:

- what physically happened;
- who recorded it;
- what each institution teaches;
- what families or local communities remember;
- which evidence is missing or politically dangerous;
- which present law, border, debt, prejudice, ritual, ruin, or resource pattern it created.

History matters when it survives as present structure. It need not conceal a twist.

## 8. World entry format

Use a compact entry, not a mandatory encyclopedia page:

```text
Name and aliases:
Status: proposal / author-confirmed / manuscript-canon / obsolete
Scope: place / state / institution / culture / economy / rule / history
Current fact:
Who experiences it differently:
Ordinary use or routine:
Constraint, cost, or enforcement:
Current story consequence:
Linked characters, events, items, and species:
Source: author note or manuscript location
Open uncertainty:
```

If an answer is unknown, leave it unknown. Never promote a plausible completion to canon.

## 9. Cross-impact audit

When adding a major fact, test only the neighboring systems it would materially affect:

- population and settlement;
- labor and prices;
- transport and communication;
- law and political power;
- warfare and security;
- family and class;
- religion and education;
- relevant items or species;
- current plot and character options.

Do not demand that every change affect all nine. Record two or three real consequences and one uncertainty. If a powerful magic rule changes nothing outside combat, either narrow the rule or explain the institutional containment.

## 10. Common failures

- Map-first design with no effect on travel, food, politics, or identity.
- A government that acts as one mind and has no administrative delay.
- Currency numbers without wages, supply, debt, or purchasing consequences.
- Every culture member behaving identically.
- Lore written as objective truth when it is actually a character or institution's belief.
- A world rule introduced only for one scene and forgotten afterward.
- Magic solving logistics while society remains inexplicably unchanged.
- Political cruelty requiring every official to be personally cruel.
- History existing only to hide a revelation.
- Proper nouns replacing distinctions the story has not yet made operational.

Repair the pressure and consequence chain before inventing more terminology.
