# Character Architecture: Core I Conception

Use this reference to create or rebuild characters, ensembles, relationship arcs, competence boundaries, moral turns, hidden sides, or opposition that grows from people rather than outline functions. It is genre-neutral and project-neutral. Store live names and facts only in the novel's Core III.

## 1. Start with evidence status

Separate what the author gave from what the design process proposes:

```text
confirmed fact:
supported inference:
trial mechanism:
open unknown:
author decision required:
```

Sparse seeds are useful. Do not complete every childhood, fear, secret, romance, trauma, habit, or ending. Add only material that produces a choice, relationship asymmetry, constraint, or later proof the requested story can use.

When importing an existing manuscript, accepted action outranks adjective cards. A person repeatedly lending money while calling themselves selfish is stronger evidence than a note saying “cold.” Preserve contradiction until the story resolves it.

## 2. Build an identity invariant

An identity invariant is what remains legible while mood, status, role, age, and pressure change. Build it from a small set:

```text
desired place among others:
value protected when time runs out:
feared confirmation from a specific relationship:
usual way of earning safety, worth, belonging, or control:
line resisted and known conditions that could move it:
self-model versus behavior others can observe:
```

Do not collapse the person into one slogan. Values have priority order and can conflict. A healer may protect life, professional authority, and a sibling; a scene becomes character-bearing when those cannot all be preserved.

Create at least one condition where the usual strategy is genuinely useful. A flaw that never helped is decoration. Create at least one condition where the same strategy transfers badly or charges someone else a price.

## 3. Give history and competence causal weight

Convert only story-relevant history through:

```text
experience → subjective lesson → trigger → first move → advantage → blind cost
```

Design competence in layers:

- **procedural transfer** — habits of checking, rehearsing, diagnosing, bargaining, repairing, studying, or leading;
- **domain knowledge** — facts, terminology, institutions, tools, bodies, or techniques actually learned;
- **authority and access** — what the person is allowed to do, sign, command, touch, enter, own, or refuse;
- **tacit local knowledge** — customs and cues outsiders do not gain from intelligence alone;
- **stress boundary** — where pain, shame, urgency, audience, or fatigue changes performance;
- **transfer error** — where a true skill is applied to the wrong category.

This prevents the expert from becoming omniscient and lets a novice possess local advantages. For isekai, time travel, social mobility, or career change, distinguish portable reasoning from unavailable data, language, law, status, equipment, and embodied practice.

## 4. Map subjective options

Plot choice begins with the options the character can actually see and bear, not the options listed by the author.

```text
live objective:
visible options:
option never noticed and why:
option noticed but socially or morally unbearable:
risk overvalued:
risk undervalued:
debt, loyalty, shame, image, or duty changing the price:
choice that looks best from inside:
cost the reader may foresee first:
```

A person may choose badly without becoming stupid. The choice fails when they forget recent knowledge, lose established competence, or ignore an obvious option they routinely use without a new pressure.

Keep alternatives alive in the design. If every route but one is secretly impossible, the decision has no character content.

## 5. Design cast ecology

A cast ecology is a network of futures, dependencies, permissions, and interference—not a roster of contrasting adjectives.

For each recurring member, keep only:

```text
future pursued without the protagonist:
resource, permission, knowledge, or relationship they control:
person or institution they depend on:
loss they price differently from the others:
strategy that intrudes on another member's plan:
offscreen next move:
```

Characters may overlap in age, job, power, temperament, or goal. Distinguish what each protects, what help costs them, whom they may disappoint, how they use the shared skill, and which failure they cannot tolerate.

Use a collision check: choose one pressure and let every member act once from their own future. Their actions should not form a neat wheel around the protagonist. At least one should be absent, late, indifferent, obstructive, or busy with a consequence the protagonist does not yet value.

Do not give equal page time, one speech each, or a private subplot to prove equality. Weight belongs to causal influence and reader promise.

## 6. Design directional relationship arcs

A relationship is a changing contract of permission, dependence, interpretation, and repair. Record both directions:

```text
what A gives B / what B gives A:
what each believes the exchange means:
who may order, interrupt, touch, represent, tease, refuse, leave, or conceal:
shared premise and disputed memory:
ordinary repair after minor conflict:
promise or boundary that would change the contract:
```

Build a relationship arc through visible contract changes:

1. **working exchange** — both receive enough to stay;
2. **unequal cost appears** — one pays more or defines the exchange differently;
3. **choice changes permission** — a secret, representation, rescue, refusal, or betrayal alters what may be asked;
4. **failed repair** — the old apology, meal, joke, order, or touch no longer works;
5. **new contract** — closeness, distance, cooperation, dependence, or hostility resumes under different terms.

Do not use a single trust score. Two people can trust combat skill and distrust judgment; permit physical closeness and refuse confession; cooperate publicly while withdrawing private representation.

## 7. Generate renewable conflict

Conflict renews when characters pursue incompatible futures using strategies that alter one another's options.

```text
A future + A strategy + A blind cost
meets
B future + B strategy + B blind cost
under an institution, resource, deadline, witness, or body constraint
→ local result → adaptation → new option map
```

The opponent need not be evil or symmetrical. Give them enough independent information, resources, authority, and loss to act before the protagonist. Let them adapt to observed behavior, not to the outline's planned chapter count.

A good local success proves competence while changing the next problem. It may attract oversight, create debt, reveal a method, divide allies, consume a resource, or make the opponent choose a harsher but rational route.

Misunderstanding can contribute, but durable conflict needs incompatible action or cost. If one honest conversation solves everything without either side surrendering a valued future, the conflict was informational rather than structural.

## 8. Earn moral turns and hidden sides

For a line the person may cross, specify:

```text
value the line currently protects:
replacement strategies that have worked:
pressures that make each replacement costlier:
visible alternative still available at the turn:
why the character cannot accept that alternative now:
choice cost tied to the protected value:
afterward: self-explanation, relationship judgment, institution, body, or habit change
```

A hidden side should share an invariant with the visible side. Tenderness and severity may both protect order, family, autonomy, reputation, or survival under different thresholds. Seed choices that can later be reinterpreted; do not seed generic ominous smiles, unexplained competence, or repeated “there is more to them” narration.

The reveal should add meaning without declaring all earlier behavior false. If the person was truly kind, the later cruelty must coexist with that kindness and make its boundary newly visible.

## 9. Build a long-form character arc

An arc is strategy change with memory, not a sequence of stronger feelings.

Useful movements:

```text
old strategy succeeds
→ same strategy charges a new cost
→ character explains failure through old model
→ improved old strategy partially succeeds
→ pressure blocks it
→ costly alternative is tried badly
→ relapse under a different trigger
→ later choice proves a changed priority
→ new strategy creates a new blind spot
```

Do not require every arc to heal, redeem, corrupt, or complete. Flat arcs may deepen commitment and alter the world. Regression arcs may increase skill while narrowing moral options. Relationship arcs and competence arcs can move independently.

Measure change through available actions, accepted costs, relationship permissions, and self-explanation. Do not rely on a monologue saying the person has changed.

## 10. Calibrate across genres

Keep the identity invariant; change the action surface supplied by the genre:

| Genre pressure | Character architecture question |
|---|---|
| School / youth | Who sees the failure, who controls belonging, and what minor act carries lasting embarrassment? |
| Romance / family | What care, dependence, representation, privacy, or future commitment can each person permit? |
| Mystery / occult | What does training make the person notice, what evidence do they discount, and what truth threatens a relationship? |
| Cultivation / progression | Which weakness remains outside the growth axis, what resource creates debt, and who gains authority from proof? |
| Isekai / time travel | Which procedure transfers, which local fact or permission does not, and what old self-model survives the new body? |
| Political / workplace | Who may decide, record, sign, delay, expose, or shift blame, and what private loyalty distorts formal duty? |
| War / survival | What is protected when information, time, food, mobility, and moral alternatives collapse? |

Genre convention supplies pressure and available action. It does not supply personal motive. Do not paste one genre's conflict scale into another.

## 11. Output contracts

Match the requested artifact:

- **character card** — evidence status, invariant, competence, residue, current relationships, open unknowns;
- **cast design** — ecology, dependencies, collisions, offscreen futures, weighting;
- **relationship design** — directional contract, break condition, failed repair, new contract;
- **arc** — old strategy, pressure sequence, option changes, proof, relapse, residue;
- **plot branch** — subjective options, different costs, downstream adaptations;
- **diagnosis** — exact interchangeable or forced layer, evidence, smallest redesign.

Do not expose every internal field unless the author asks for a workshop. Keep proposals non-canonical.

## 12. Conception tests

1. **Evidence** — which detail is confirmed, inferred, trial, or unknown?
2. **Invariant** — what remains recognizable across fear, authority, intimacy, and success?
3. **Choice** — are at least two options genuinely visible and differently priced?
4. **Competence** — what transfers, what requires local knowledge, and where does authority stop?
5. **Cast ecology** — whose future continues without the protagonist?
6. **Relationship arc** — which permission or dependency changes through a choice?
7. **Conflict** — do both sides adapt and alter one another's options?
8. **Moral turn** — was the old boundary useful, pressured, crossed at cost, and remembered?
9. **Genre** — did the setting change available action without replacing motive?
10. **Name-swap** — can another cast member inherit the same future, option map, and cost unchanged?

If a deeper test fails, do not compensate with quirks, biography volume, catchphrases, or a dramatic reveal.
