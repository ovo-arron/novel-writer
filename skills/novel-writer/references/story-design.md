Use this reference for premises, character systems, conflicts, compact outlines, arcs, and chapter plans. For whole-story causal graphs, multi-thread weaving, volume inheritance, sequences, and reverse outlines, continue into `structural-architecture.md`. Design enough structure to create choices; do not design so much that characters can only execute a prewritten argument.

## 1. A premise that can keep producing story

A durable premise contains five interacting parts:

| Part | Question |
|---|---|
| Human pressure | What does the protagonist need before the fantasy concept matters? |
| Distinctive mechanism | What rule, position, ability, institution, or transformation changes ordinary life? |
| Cost | What does using or refusing the mechanism consume? |
| Opposition | Who benefits from the current order or wants a different future? |
| Renewable question | What question can return in harder forms instead of being answered once? |

Weak premise: “A student reincarnates as a demon and becomes the strongest.”

Stronger engine: “A gifted student reincarnates as a physically weak minor demon in a state preparing for another human invasion. She must learn language, work, loyalty, and war from childhood while former classmates grow up inside the opposing civilization. Every gain in belonging makes the eventual reunion more dangerous.”

The second version produces learning, family, class, state, identity, friendship, and war conflicts without requiring a cheat ability.

## 2. Character design through recurring choices

Give each major character a compact dynamic card:

- **visible want**: what they can admit wanting;
- **private need**: what they hope another person will give or confirm;
- **competence**: where their judgment has earned authority;
- **blind spot**: where that competence misleads them;
- **social mask**: how they want others to read them;
- **pressure behavior**: what they protect first when time runs out;
- **relationship asymmetry**: what they can ask from one person but not another;
- **change test**: a later choice that would prove genuine growth.

Avoid trait bundles such as “cold, smart, kind.” A character becomes legible when the same pressure produces a recognizable but not perfectly predictable choice.

## 2A. Character-to-event bridge

Before inventing an incident for a major character, connect:

```text
practical need + desired image with this listener
→ familiar strategy → independent resistance
→ feared confirmation becomes plausible
→ choice that creates cost or obligation
```

The familiar strategy should have protected something before. Plot pressure exposes its limit; it does not make the person forget obvious alternatives. A reliable character may hide a problem because finishing it alone protects the image of reliability. A cautious character may take a worse route because asking permission would confirm dependence. These are causal shapes, not reusable scenes.

Relationships generate plot when permission changes action. Record who may ask, interrupt, refuse, touch, borrow, accuse, leave, or be believed. A relationship scene advances when one of those permissions is exercised, withdrawn, abused, misread, or publicly tested—not merely when two people discuss how they feel.

Across an arc, repeat the pressure in changed conditions. Track which strategy the character uses, what it protects, what it costs, what they learn or mislearn, and how another actor adapts. The later proof of change is a choice whose price the earlier character would have refused.

## 3. Opposition with its own future

An antagonist, rival, institution, or opposing society needs:

1. a future it is trying to create;
2. a reason that future appears necessary from inside its knowledge;
3. resources and constraints independent of the protagonist;
4. internal disagreement;
5. a price for victory.

Do not make every opponent secretly admire the protagonist. Do not make institutional evil depend on every member being personally cruel. Systems become convincing when ordinary duties reproduce harm.

## 4. Conflict as incompatible action

Conflict is not two positions stated aloud. It is two actions that cannot both succeed under the same conditions.

Useful conflict sources:

- two people need the same scarce resource;
- protection requires control, while dignity requires autonomy;
- truth helps one relationship and destroys another;
- speed saves lives but loses legitimacy;
- a rule is fair in general and disastrous in this case;
- competence in one culture becomes misconduct in another;
- winning the immediate battle worsens the strategic war.

Escalate by changing conditions, not merely raising volume. A later conflict should add cost, uncertainty, public exposure, divided loyalty, or irreversible timing.

## 5. Scene engine

Draft each necessary scene as a causal movement:

```text
entry state → pressure becomes concrete → character attempts something
→ resistance answers → choice or failure → changed exit state → residue
```

The exit state need not be a twist. It can be a smaller certainty, a missed chance, a new obligation, a changed routine, or a relationship made slightly harder.

Run two tests before keeping a scene:

- **deletion test**: if the scene disappears, what later action, knowledge, cost, or relationship becomes impossible to explain?
- **swap test**: could another character, room, lesson, or errand perform the same function unchanged? If yes, restore character ownership or compress it.

Daily life may stay even when it does not advance the central plot, provided it establishes a habit, dependency, material condition, or relationship texture the story actually uses.

## 6. Information design

Classify information by when it becomes actionable:

- **needed now**: without it, the present choice is incomprehensible;
- **felt now, named later**: readers can observe an effect before receiving the rule;
- **known by character, irrelevant now**: omit until pressure calls it forward;
- **unknown to viewpoint**: show consequences, rumors, gaps, or wrong models rather than authorial explanation.

Let rules enter through failed use, successful use with a cost, bargaining, work, injury, ritual, or institutional procedure. Avoid assigning one convenient question to each supporting character so the cast can recite the setting.

## 7. Long-form architecture

### Story promise

Write one sentence that names the durable reader experience, not the ending. Example: “Watch an underestimated reincarnated demon learn a brutal world from the ground up and choose what kind of person to become before two civilizations force her old and new lives into the same war.”

### Arc ladder

Build arcs by expanding the unit of consequence:

1. body and household;
2. peers and local work;
3. institution and faction;
4. region and public identity;
5. war, state, or world order.

This is a common ladder, not a mandatory sequence. Move outward only when earlier relationships and skills create leverage at the next scale.

### Volume design

Each volume should contain:

- a visible practical objective;
- a relationship under changing pressure;
- one world rule tested in more than one context;
- a midpoint change that invalidates the easiest plan;
- a climax choice whose solution creates later residue;
- an ending that settles the volume question while opening a consequence, not merely a louder enemy.

### Chapter design

For serial fiction, every chapter needs a reason to exist, but not the same kind of hook. Useful exits include a decision, discovery, emotional reclassification, arrival, failed promise, completed task with an unexpected cost, or quiet aftermath that changes what the reader expects next.

Do not force every chapter to contain a fight, reversal, quote, new setting fact, and cliffhanger at once.

## 8. Non-canonical forecasts

When the author is uncertain, generate two to five future branches in isolation. For each branch state:

- the decisive character choice;
- the next three to five causal beats;
- what becomes newly possible;
- what the branch sacrifices;
- continuity risks;
- how well it matches the current author intent.

Label forecasts as proposals. Selecting one may update the plan, but does not retroactively make its events canon.

Record the forecast's source state, assumptions, divergence question, horizon, and creation time. Any accepted event or author correction that changes an entry assumption marks it `stale`. Re-check or regenerate a stale forecast before using it; selection changes only the candidate plan, never accepted prose or current truth.

## 9. Mechanical design smells

- Premise is a catalog of tropes without a renewable human pressure.
- Every named detail has a future payoff, making the world feel arranged for the outline.
- Supporting characters enter in the exact order needed to explain systems.
- Every setback teaches the precise lesson required for the next success.
- Symmetry is so complete that characters become diagram labels.
- The antagonist's plan depends on ignoring obvious alternatives.
- A training, shopping, lecture, or errand scene exists only because the author needs an information carrier.
- A twist changes facts but not anyone's next choice.
- Growth is announced through self-analysis instead of demonstrated by a choice the earlier character could not make.
- Character cards are complete, but the same event survives a name swap because self-image, feared confirmation, and relationship permission never affect the attempt.
- Repeated failures reset strategy, body, trust, and opponent knowledge, so the final success feels scheduled rather than learned.

Repair the causal engine before polishing sentences.
